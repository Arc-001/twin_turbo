The problem

When should a jet engine come off the wing?

Every turbofan wears out, and no two wear out at the same rate. Pull one too late and it fails in service, the most expensive outcome in aviation maintenance. Pull it too early and you throw away flight cycles you already paid for.

The classical answer is age replacement: retire every engine at a fixed age chosen from fleet statistics. It is safe, and it is wasteful, because it treats the longest-lived engine like the shortest-lived one.

This project asks whether an engine's own sensors can tell us, cycle by cycle, how much life it has left, how sure we are, and what to do about it, with no human in the loop.

Fleet-level results over 709 engines replayed from first cycle to failure (5-fold cross-validation). Every figure comes from the same traces the other views replay.

The data

NASA C-MAPSS

NASA's Commercial Modular Aero-Propulsion System Simulation runs a fleet of turbofans from healthy to failure. Each row is one engine in one flight cycle: 3 operating settings and 21 sensors (temperatures, pressures, spool speeds), all with realistic noise and unknown initial wear.

Training engines run all the way to failure. Test engines are cut off early, and the benchmark task is to predict their remaining useful life (RUL).

Four sub-datasets make the problem harder along two axes: how many flight regimes an engine sees, and how many ways it can fail.

The architecture

Two models, one decision

A stateless edge model runs on every cycle, on board. A stateful digital twin, one per engine, lives off board and is synchronized only when the edge model raises a trigger.

The policy never acts on a point estimate. It acts on a calibrated lower bound: the RUL we are 95% sure the engine still has.

Edge model

Fast, stateless, calibrated

The last 30 cycles of sensor data are compressed into four numbers per sensor (last value, mean, spread, trend) and fed to a gradient-boosted tree ensemble: 150 trees, depth 6, about 900 comparisons per prediction.

A point estimate alone is not enough to act on, so the edge model is wrapped in Mondrian conformal prediction: its own out-of-fold errors, binned by predicted RUL, give an interval with a distribution-free 90% coverage guarantee.

Normalize
K-means finds the 6 flight regimes in FD002/FD004; each sensor is z-scored within its regime. Without this, edge RMSE on FD004 rises from 15.0 to 19.0.

What the edge model listens to
Digital twin

A particle filter per engine

The twin tracks one scalar: a health index fused from the sensors, fitted so early life scores 1 and failure scores 0. Its shape between those points comes from the data, and it is close to exponential.

Degradation is modelled as HI(t) = φ − θ·e^(βt). From 200+ run-to-failure engines we learn a fleet prior over φ, θ, β and the failure threshold. Each engine's twin starts as 2,000 draws from that prior and re-weights them with every observation of that engine alone. What survives is a belief about this specific engine, and pushing each particle forward to the threshold gives a full RUL distribution, not a single number.

Instance
One twin per engine, holding 2,000 × 4 numbers of state.
Synchronize
The edge buffers one float per cycle (the health index); a sync hands the buffer over. The result does not depend on how often it syncs.
Forecast
Closed-form crossing time per particle: t* = ln((φ − h)/θ) / β.
Calibrated
90% posterior intervals cover the truth 86–94% of the time on every dataset.

Decision layer

Escalate, fuse, act

Three triggers decide when the twin is worth consulting. When it is, the policy takes the more cautious of the two calibrated lower bounds: the twin tracks the long horizon best, the edge model is sharpest right before failure. Each bound is a one-sided 95% bound, so their minimum still holds at 90% or better.

The lower bound then selects one of four actions.

low_rul
Edge estimate at or below 60 cycles: the engine has left the clearly-safe band.
sharp_drop
Edge estimate fell by 8+ cycles in one cycle: possible fault onset.
periodic
No sync for 20 cycles: catch slow drift the edge model might mask.

The twin assimilates each engine's full observed history, while the windowed models see only the last 30 cycles. That persistent per-engine state is what makes it a twin, and it is also why the comparison is not like-for-like.
